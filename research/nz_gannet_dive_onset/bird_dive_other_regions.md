# Marine bird attacks, prey response and the vertical axis

## Røst, Norway — herring responding to Atlantic puffins

Axelsen et al. observed 104 juvenile herring schools south of Røst on 10–15 July 1996. Twenty-one schools were attacked, 18 by puffins. During 153 minutes of analysed video, 27 schools showed 83 changes of shape or grouping across all predator contexts. Removing overlapping episodes gave 33 min 35 s in these altered configurations, or 21.95% of observation time. Shape responses occurred 1.48 times/min during puffin dives and 0.57 times/min when birds swam at the surface (Results; Discussion rounds the latter to 0.58). That is about 2.6 times as frequent. Mean response durations were 42.3 s (SE 10.8) and 46.5 s (SE 9.9), respectively. Repeated events occurred within schools. The authors sought events adaptively. [Original paper, Tables 1–2, Figs. 4–7](https://doi.org/10.1139/z01-113) · [Full article text](https://www.academia.edu/18325191/Pretty_patterns_but_a_simple_strategy_predator_prey_interactions_between_juvenile_herring_and_Atlantic_puffins_observed_with_multibeam_sonar)

The transducer viewed a 90° horizontal sector and integrated across 15° vertically; the observer occasionally tilted it to look deeper. Figure 4 presents plan views, including a puffin entry and exit, while Figure 7 gives duration by response type. The vertical axis in the plan view is range in the horizontal field. Its distances cannot be digitized as school depths. Table 1 contrasts different sets of schools, with mean horizontal areas of 505 m² during dives, 203 m² with surface birds and 87 m² with no predators. Variable initial school size and visibility are relevant to interpreting the difference. These results provide independent, timed field observations against which a Boids model can compare group deformation. The attack effect on near-surface residence is unidentified. [Instrument, sampling and results](https://doi.org/10.1139/z01-113)

## One Tree Reef, Australia — depth-limited escape

At low tide a school of hardyheads was observed for 13 periods and 37 foraging bouts from above, with 362 lesser crested tern dives. Fourteen additional underwater observations of dives provided a side perspective. The school was stationary between dives. On water entry the fish nearest the path moved laterally, fish below reacted in succession, a gap formed around the bird and the school regrouped after the bird resurfaced. This sequence occurred on all 362 dives viewed from above. During the underwater session the diver rested at 0.63 m, and terns reached 0.43–0.58 m. The authors describe a shallow bed that constrained downward escape. They measured the bird's shallowest and deepest dive for the session. Figure 1 is a response diagram and Table 1 holds observation-period summaries. No attack-level fish-depth trace was reported. A layer threshold of 5 m would include the entire water column observed by the diver, throughout both phases. This site supplies a natural depth constraint and a repeated horizontal-response case. [Primary paper, Methods, Fig. 1 and Table 1](https://www.marineornithology.org/PDF/49_1/49_1_159-166.pdf)

## Barkley Sound, Canada — species-specific flock persistence

Chilton and Sealy recorded 108 mixed-species feeding flocks from shore, June–October 1983. Table 2 reports the following bird-flock durations. Differences are means for flocks with the species present minus means for flocks without it. Seven fish schools were already ascending with nearby birds at rest. Longer-lasting flocks allow more opportunities for a species to arrive. Species attendance overlaps across contrasts. Neither the end of flock activity nor participation by a late arrival identifies a prey school crossing a depth threshold. ## Other independent evidence

- **Scotland, Small Isles.** In September 2004 the author observed common guillemots approaching shoals from below or from their lower margin on underwater film. Manx shearwaters approached mainly from above or the upper sides. The author predicts opposing effects on shoal depth and describes other possible causes of ascents. Matched depth measurements and event-by-event times are absent from this account. For a directional model the measured observation is the predator's side of approach. [Original field account, chapter 3, pp. 126–127](https://etheses.dur.ac.uk/2153/1/2153_161.PDF)
- **Gulf of Alaska.** A field report describes schools sounding as a feeding flock develops. Its authors explicitly infer much of the behaviour of fish from gulls ceasing to feed or switching position. Schools also moved vertically before flocks had formed. No direct paired prey-depth series accompanies this account. [Wiens et al., original report, pp. 152–154](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-I-59ba417fddb5b05472b171d0cfddbe0b/pdf/GOVPUB-I-59ba417fddb5b05472b171d0cfddbe0b.pdf)
- **Washington, San Juan Channel.** Zamon found that tidal flood phases coincided with more bird feeding and more frequent acoustic encounters with fish. Hydroacoustic transects took 8–10 min and measured approximately 30 m horizontal by 1 m vertical bins, including 2–12 m surface-layer analyses; shore observers counted feeding flocks over 30 min. This supplies a measured common environmental driver. Matched, continuously tracked individual schools under individual bird attacks were outside the sampling design. [Original study](https://www.int-res.com/articles/meps2003/261/m261p243.pdf)
- **Japan, Teuri Island.** Dryad provides 20.57 GB of rhinoceros auklet accelerometry, depth-temperature text files and instrument data in four years (2012, 2013, 2019, 2020). The pressure sensors travelled on birds. The archive documents how bird dives differed between anchovy- and sand-lance-dominated years, with no corresponding fish-school depth channel. [Primary dataset and exact file list](https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.7sqv9s4x8)
- **Argentina, Punta Cuevas.** A fledgling Magellanic penguin was observed at high tide in 1.5–2.5 m of water. On four pursuits, individual fish swam upwards and jumped repeatedly, with one confirmed capture and three outcomes unknown. The penguin mostly ignored polarized schools, approaching within about 1 m at times, and the schools bunched and swam away. The four surface escapes are observations of individual prey, and the 17 timed dives measure the bird. [Wilson 1996, field observations](https://www.marineornithology.org/PDF/24/24_14.pdf)

None of these sources supplied an independent paired estimate of change in the same marine fish school's metric depth or cumulative seconds in a shallow layer after an individual diving-bird attack. Records of fish collectively changing shape, arrival of prey at the surface, and species-specific flock duration support separate predictions for a predator–prey extension of Boids.
